Teaching

Context Statement

I am a Cognitive Neuroscientist in the Psychology and Neuroscience department at Duke University. My primary research focuses on understanding the neural and behavioral underpinnings of cognitive control over our ability to switch between perception and memory.

My teaching practices are inspired by my previous and current mentors and are grounded in principles of accessibility, transparency, and accountability in science, with a strong emphasis on mentoring students from underrepresented backgrounds. I have served as instructor , discussion section and lab leader, and guest lecturer across multiple undergraduate courses ( Cognitive Neuroscience, Research Methods, Python Programming).

Across all teaching roles, I strive to promote accountability, creativity, and healthy skepticism through active learning, discussion-based instruction, and collaborative practices that encourage students to engage in scientific discourse within open and supportive environments.

Designing and Planning for Learning Activities

My approach to designing and planning learning activities is guided by the belief that students learn most effectively when they are active participants in the process of scientific inquiry, rather than passive recipients of information. Drawing on my experience engaging in the scientific process, I have designed activities that make students active participants in that process including hypothesis generation, critical evaluation of evidence, and iterative problem-solving in a way that is accessible for students of all backgrounds, with the express aim to provide them with the tools to engage in research in the future. For example, as a python instructor for undergraduate students in the Cognitive Neuroscience Research Internship (CNRI) program at Duke University, I, alongside other Cog Neuro colleagues [V5], have developed a series of hands-on programming exercises/projects that allow students to apply programming concepts to real-world applications that they would encounter in the research setting. One of the most difficult steps for undergraduate students to take on their scientific journey is the first step. This course is specifically designed to provide young underrepresented scientists with the experience needed to join a research lab [V2].

The course typically runs across 11 weeks, and has been designed to help students build up an understanding of basic python programming from basic syntax to more complex structures while applying this knowledge towards more research oriented endeavors such as developing cognitive tasks, analyzing data, and visualizing acquired data. Consisting of 8 to 10 students each semester, we begin the course by having students complete programming exercises in a flipped-classroom format, primarily engaging in small groups [K2]. By doing so, we are able to give hands-on experience and real-time feedback as they start to learn how to engage with python [V2]. After covering the programming basics, we transition towards explicitly working with PyschoPy, a commonly used python package and GUI for creating cognitive tasks, and switch our focus to more research oriented uses of python [K4]. The course includes a semester-long project, geared towards providing students with a means of applying what they have learned in class. For the project, students propose a research idea and then develop a cognitive task to test the idea, culminating in a testable task that they use to gather data from their fellow colleagues in the course and then present the results as a final project [K1]. Across the semester, we embed several checkins, allowing us to meet the students at their level throughout the semester [V2]. The projects have been designed to build alongside their increasing understanding of programming, so as they learn increasing levels of python they can express their ideas. With this course, I hope to not simply provide students with tools, but a platform for them to express their ideas.

Teaching and Supporting Student Learning

Teaching my own course has been a journey full of many rewarding experiences. However, some of the most transformative experiences for me have come as a discussion section leader for several undergraduate courses: Cognitive neuroscience and Research methods. As a teaching assistant for cognitive neuroscience I made use of several forms of media in order to actively engage students, from having them listen to podcasts on happiness to watching Ted talks on the formation of language or completing widely used cognitive tasks [K4]. Students worked in smaller discussion groups prior to contributing to larger class discussions as a means of helping them develop and be more confident in their ideas, promoting more equitable participation [K2]. It’s clear that cognitive neuroscience can be seen as a rather daunting course, with dense readings and complex material. To this end, I found it important to provide students with several forms of engaging activities that showcase the complex ideas in an easier, more relaxed fashion. For example, as a way of understanding social cognition concepts I had students play the game Codenames, then discuss the strategies they used, relating them to key concepts such as perspective taking. Another subject students find rather difficult, sometimes much more difficult, is statistics.

As a teaching assistant for Research Methods it’s been a growing experience helping students find the joy in statistics. Taking part in weekly meetings with the teaching team we aimed to make statistics more accessible by bringing real world examples to the sections for students to engage with [V5]. Having students work with real world data made it easier for them to conceptualize the material and work with commonly used statistics software like JASP [K4]. Being that statistics can often feel like a cold and static subject, we also wanted to make the class itself feel more friendly, so we aimed to learn everybody's name by the second week [V1]. We also shifted away from simple verbal explanations of different behavioral relationships to more visual formats. By having individuals draw the graphical relationships on whiteboard in groups we gave students the opportunity to become more comfortable representing relational ideas as graphs, a common practice in the field [V1]. It’s a beautiful moment watching students explain their idea, then after asking them to draw it on a graph seeing them actively have an ‘Aha’ moment as they realize something they missed before. To match individual students' learning pace and to make sure no students were struggling, I incorporated a feedback survey at the end of each section asking about pace, what worked well, and any points of confusion [V2]. This experience was valuable to me as a way of developing my teaching flexibility and being able to bring complex topics to more accessible states for future scientists.

Supporting and Guiding Learners

Outside the classroom, I have had the privilege of being able to mentor several undergraduate research assistants. Focusing on RA’s from underrepresented backgrounds, I recognize that research can seem like a black box from the outside in. I also believe the best way to learn about scientific research is to actively engage in the research process. For these reasons, I typically assign my RA’s to a project and have them take part in every stage: task/survey design, task creation, data collection, data analysis, and interpretation of results. I also make it a point to meet on a weekly basis and leave room for open discussion on what I call “Career Development” where they can ask questions not directly related to the research, but more towards the general field as a whole or perhaps different aspects of grad school [V4, K2]. I make it clear to my mentees that I am here to help them learn the skills that they feel are important. I also make it clear that scientific research is hard, and most ideas will fail. The important thing is to learn how to be process-oriented. Science after all is defined by failures.

Integrate & Reflect

My teaching is grounded in a commitment to accessibility, transparency, and inclusion. Across course design, classroom instruction, assessment, and mentoring, I aim to make scientific learning active, collaborative, and approachable, especially for students who may not initially see themselves as belonging in research spaces. I try to create environments where students can ask questions freely, test ideas openly, and learn through doing. I view teaching not only as the transmission of disciplinary knowledge, but as an opportunity to help students build curiosity and resilience to challenges. My broader teaching philosophy is that students learn best when they are treated as emerging scholars: capable of critical thought, creative problem-solving, and meaningful contributions to scientific discourse. Beyond content knowledge, I want students to leave my courses with a stronger sense of curiosity and scientific possibility.

Looking Ahead

As I continue to refine my pedagogy, I always look forward to opportunities for me to develop and create new courses. Science is continually evolving and with it so should our way of teaching it. In recent years the scientific world has shifted towards more open and reproducible scientific practices and many seminal works have failed to reproduce. It would be a dream to teach a class centered on open science and the wonderful work that metascience has brought us. Personally, I have always found the field of metascience to be incredibly empowering because it forces the individual to accept that science is not perfect and should be scrutinized. I would love to bring this same empowerment to young scientists in the future!
